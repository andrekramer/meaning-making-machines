Alice and the Pills
A Perceptive Descent into whirls of Emptiness and Fullness



Alice is offered two pills.

One is marked P.

The other is marked NP.

She is not told whether the second means Not Process, Non-Process, or something more computationally troublesome. Alice takes P.

P stands for Process, though the letter also recalls p, momentum. The suggestion is simple: stop asking first where a thing is, yet alone what it is ultimately made off, and ask instead how it moves, changes, persists and relates.

Nothing vanishes when Alice swallows it. The table remains a table; the horse remains a horse. But solidity becomes provisional. A table can be redescribed as a continuing balance of forces and materials. A cell as metabolism and regulation. A horse as an enduring organization of body, movement, memory and environment. Alice herself as perception, retention, expectation and response.

The first surprise is that Emptiness does not mean absence. A whirlpool exists, yet no particular molecule of water is the whirlpool. Its identity persists through the movement rather than through fixed material constituents. Cells, organisms and selves may be similar in this respect.

To say that a form is empty is therefore not to deny its reality. It is to deny that its identity belongs to it entirely in isolation.

Emptiness: no form has an independent essence.

Alice tries to get beneath the process and finds another process. She changes scale and finds relations, flows and transformations where she expected a final object. Perhaps there is no smallest solid thing waiting at the bottom of the rabbit hole.

Here she encounters Fullness.

Fullness: nothing lies outside the relational whole.

The opposition between Fullness and Emptiness begins to collapse. Emptiness says that no form stands alone. Fullness says that every form belongs to a larger relational field. A thing may be empty precisely because it is full of relations.

“That is full, this is full,
From that fullness comes this fullness,
If you take away this fullness from that fullness,
Only fullness remains.” [1]

The Upanishadic intuition of fullness need not therefore mean an enormous substance containing smaller substances. It can mean that differentiation never exhausts the whole. Buddhist emptiness approaches from the opposite direction: none of those differentiations is self-sufficient.

One says:

The other:

Nothing within the whole stands alone.

Huxley offers another metaphor for the same territory: the reducing valve and the ego-filter. Alice pushes the image one step further.

The doors are made of perception.

Perception may not merely pass through a boundary between self and world. It may help generate the distinctions of inside and outside, subject and object, Alice and Wonderland.

The ego-filter does not simply stand between two independently existing sides. It helps stabilize one side as me and the other as world.

The Māṇḍūkya Upanishad then gives Alice an unexpectedly old map of changing experiential structure.

There is waking.

There is dreaming.

There is deep sleep.

And there is the fourth quarter.

Waking fixes distinctions into a stable world. Dreaming reveals how easily they can loosen: people, times, places and identities flow into one another without experience simply ceasing. Deep sleep removes the ordinary differentiated scene.

Then the headset performs its strangest trick.

It begins to notice itself.

An organism ordinarily detects changes in the world. But suppose a neurotoxin changes the machinery through which the world is being perceived. Survival may now depend upon recognizing something more subtle:

Even then, the wearer of the headset remains unexplained. Alice has perhaps found a way to describe self-monitoring experience without yet explaining why there is an experiencer at all.

By now she has travelled surprisingly far.

Objects have become processes. Processes have become relations. Relations have opened into possible transitions. Probability has appeared where continuous movement is divided into forms. Memory has entered the present as retained history. The perceived world has become a headset. The boundary between subject and object has turned out to be something perception itself may help construct.

And when Alice applies the same operation to experience, even qualia look less like objects and more like temporary differentiations of process.

At every stage, the P-pill performs the same transformation:

→

.

Emptiness says that no resulting form is final.

Fullness says that no resulting process stands outside the whole.

Bohm’s holomovement suggests that perhaps Alice was never passing through truly separate levels at all. She may have been moving among different abstractions from one undivided movement.

She therefore returns without a theory of everything or an explanation of consciousness.

What has changed is the question.

Instead of:

What is everything made of?

Alice asks:

What is moving, how is it differentiating, and at what grain have I mistaken that movement for a thing?

The P-pill is not an answer. It is a way of loosening the grip of objects long enough to see what else might be there.

And the fourth quarter leaves Alice with one final warning.

Not Process.

Not Substance.

Not Fullness.

Not Emptiness.

Every apparent bottom may be another door.

And the doors are made of perception.
