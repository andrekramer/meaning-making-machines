The Ever Dividing Whole
How Difference Might Become World, Time and Self



“This subtle part is what all this has as self. It is truth: it is the self. You are that, Śvetaketu” The sage Uddālaka Āruṇi instructs his son Śvetaketu (श्वेतकेतु).
— Chāndogya Upaniṣad (छान्दोग्य उपनिषद्)

The central image should not be a fixed whole. It should be a whole that keeps dividing without ever becoming merely a collection of separate parts.

If the dividing is part of how the whole exists, then division is not a fall from unity, nor a mechanical fragmentation of substance. It is closer to a grammar of manifestation.

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad gives a remarkably suggestive image: what was unmanifest becomes manifest through name and form. Yet the Upanishadic whole is not thereby exhausted by what appears. The old invocation says:

That is fullness. This is fullness.

From fullness, fullness comes.

Take fullness from fullness, and fullness remains.


This is strange arithmetic.

A fraction is not necessarily a slice cut from a cake. A quarter can be a stance, a mode, a way in which the whole appears.

That strangeness is part of what interests me.

The Upanishads repeatedly mix what modern thought separates: quantity and quality, inner and outer, physical and mental, part and whole. Directions, worlds, fire, food, speech, breath, sun and mind can occupy parallel positions within the same formal pattern.

The same template returns with different contents.

Something quantitative seems to be happening, but the terms are not quantities of a homogeneous substance.

That is the productive blur.

A Changing Hypercube
I have previously used the image of a hypercube of opposites: a multidimensional space organised around paired differences such as

inside/outside

forward/backwards

past/future

hot/cold

hunger/satiation

left/right

self/world

The hypercube was never meant to be fixed. What now interests me is that it may be less abstract than it first appeared. Its oppositions can emerge as physical, biological and felt differences: left and right, inside and outside, hunger and satiation, past and future.

Its dimensions can emerge. food / fire

They can disappear. thirst / water

They can couple to one another. mind / breath

A distinction that matters to one form of life may not exist, in any meaningful sense, for another.

A rock does not inhabit hunger and satiation.

An organism does.

A creature with memory acquires a different relation to past and future.

A creature capable of representation acquires distinctions between the actual, remembered, imagined and possible.

The hypercube changes with the thing whose world it describes.

Perhaps more strongly: the world of the thing changes as new differences become available to it.

So the hypercube is not simply a state-space.

It is a changing space of salience.

Its axes are not neutral coordinates. They are tensions along which a temporary whole must steer if it is to remain itself.

Name, Form and Action
This makes the Upanishadic triad of name, form and action—nāma, rūpa, karma—particularly suggestive.

Not as a literal causal theory, but read through this lens:

difference
→
form
→
name
→
action
→
new difference
.

Form distinguishes.

Name makes a distinction available to representation.

Action carries the distinction forward and alters the conditions under which the next distinctions arise.

The space continually remakes itself.

The whole does not divide once.

It keeps differentiating.

The Strange Geometry of the Quarter
The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad gives another peculiar example.

Ātman is described as having four quarters or feet:

waking,

dreaming,

deep sleep,

and the “fourth,” Turīya.

But Turīya is not simply a fourth equal compartment beside the other three.

It is not 4 quaters adding up to 1. “Not this; Not this.”

The “fourth” is better understood as that through which the other states are known or made intelligible.

That suggests another way of thinking about the hypercube.

Perhaps Ātman is not another coordinate within it.

Not another axis.

Not even its geometric centre.

Perhaps it names the speculative invariant through the transformations.

The cube changes.

The perspective changes.

Waking becomes dream, dream becomes sleep, sleep becomes waking.

But something about the possibility of experience appears continuous through those changes.

That does not prove anything about consciousness.

But it is a powerful lens.

Breakout: The Fourth Quadrant
Awake, Dream, Sleep — and Neither
Taking the Māṇḍūkya Upanishad division of experience into four quarters.

Awake: the world appears divided and stable. Self and other, inside and outside, past and future seem clearly separated.

Dream: those distinctions remain, but become fluid. People, places, times and identities can merge or change without the dream immediately collapsing. Dreaming reveals that many oppositions we treat as fixed are constructed and surprisingly plastic.

Deep sleep: even those distinctions disappear. There is no ordinary subject confronting an object, no dream-world, no apparent division.

Then comes the fourth, turīya. But it is not simply another state beyond the first three. The Upanishad describes it through negation: not outward-knowing, not inward-knowing, not both; not knowing, but also not unknowing.

Its logic is therefore not:

A
→
B
→
C
→
D

but something closer to:

A
,
¬
A
,
not merely either
.

Waking presents opposites. Dreaming loosens them. Deep sleep dissolves them. The fourth then refuses to identify reality either with the opposites or with their absence.

This is the deeper force of neti, neti — not this; not that. It does not merely choose a middle position between two poles. It questions whether the opposition itself exhausts what is possible.

The fourth quadrant is therefore less another point on the map than a challenge to the map itself.

A similar mistake may occur when we treat intelligence and consciousness as though they necessarily rise together.

Human beings present them as a tightly coupled package. Greater intelligence normally appears alongside richer awareness, memory, agency, language and social understanding. It is therefore tempting to imagine a single scale:

less mind
⟶
more mind

with intelligence and consciousness increasing together.

But perhaps this is another opposition or coupling that should be questioned.

Bostrom’s orthogonality argument separates intelligence from goals: a system can become more intelligent without becoming morally better or acquiring any particular end. The same move can be made with consciousness:

I
⊥
C

Intelligence and consciousness may be independent dimensions rather than two names for increasing degrees of mind.

This produces apparently strange possibilities:

high intelligence
+
low consciousness

or

low intelligence
+
rich consciousness
.

They seem strange largely because biological evolution has bundled the two together in us.

The Māṇḍūkya suggests a useful caution about such conceptual pairings. Waking and sleeping initially appear to be simple opposites. Dreaming complicates the division. Deep sleep complicates it again. The fourth quarter finally refuses both “conscious” and “unconscious” as adequate descriptions.

The lesson is not that turīya explains artificial intelligence. It is methodological: when two concepts appear naturally joined or opposed, do not assume that the structure belongs to reality rather than to our way of organising experience.

So instead of asking:

Is an intelligent machine conscious?

as though intelligence naturally tends toward consciousness, we might first ask whether the two belong on the same scale at all.

Perhaps:

intelligence
≠
consciousness

just as:

awake
≠
conscious
,
asleep
≠
unconscious

turn out to be much less straightforward than they initially appear.

And the stronger Upanishadic challenge remains in the background. If consciousness itself can be analysed into waking, dreaming, deep sleep and finally something described as neither conscious nor unconscious in the ordinary sense, then “consciousness” may not even be a single clean axis.

The orthogonality thesis may therefore be only an intermediate move:

first separate intelligence from consciousness

and then:

ask whether consciousness itself is the right coordinate
.

The fourth quarter does not give us an answer to the AI-consciousness problem. It gives us something potentially more valuable: a warning not to mistake a familiar pair of concepts for the dimensions along which reality itself must be divided.

Homochirality: 
When Difference Acquires a History
Homochirality gives this otherwise abstract picture an unexpectedly physical image.

Chemistry permits left- and right-handed molecular forms, yet life became overwhelmingly handed.

At some point a symmetry was broken.

More importantly, the resulting difference was retained:

The alternative has not disappeared from physics.

That may be the important image.

A difference occurs.

Free Energy, Entropy and Work
Here I mean free energy in the broad thermodynamic sense rather than primarily Friston’s active inference or predictive-processing framework.

Energy permits transformation.

Entropy reflects the multiplicity of possible arrangements.

Free energy is what remains available for organised work under constraints.

So a living system does not simply “reduce entropy.”

It continually uses energy flows to maintain distinctions that matter to its continued existence.

Inside from outside.

Food from poison.

Viable temperature from destructive temperature.

Integrity from dissolution.

A living whole does not eliminate difference.

It maintains some differences and acts upon others.

That gives free energy a modest but useful place in the picture:

energy gradients
→
work
→
maintenance of constraints
→
continued differentiation
.

Possibility becomes persistent difference.

Homochirality is one striking image of this.

A cell is another.

An organism another.

Perhaps some collectives—colonies, cultures, institutions, ecosystems—can become temporary wholes of their own.

Not necessarily conscious wholes.

But wholes whose parts and boundaries make sense partly through the organisation they sustain.

The Inception of “For-ness”
Something important happens when a difference is no longer merely a difference in the world but a difference for a whole.

A chemical gradient can be described externally.

For an organism, that gradient can become:

toward / away

food / poison

better / worse

This is where the hypercube becomes an engine of salience.

The axes acquire direction.

Some states are viable.

Others are dangerous.

Some differences matter because they threaten the persistence of the organisation whose world they belong to.

This still does not give us consciousness.

But it gives us something like for-ness.

A difference has become significant relative to a whole.

Perhaps that is one of the steps between mere physical asymmetry and lived orientation.

Breakout: Poison or Protection?
My earlier hypothesis about plant toxins and the evolution of awareness fits strangely well here.

A neuroactive substance can create a peculiar evolutionary problem: has the world changed, or have I changed? Detecting that difference may favour organisms able to monitor not only their surroundings but the reliability of their own perceptual machinery.

DMT adds an intriguing complication. It is profoundly psychedelic, yet preclinical studies also suggest neuroprotective effects under hypoxic and ischemic stress, possibly involving the sigma-1 receptor.

So even the category toxin begins to blur.

From outside we see receptors, cellular stress, calcium signalling and survival. From inside there may be radically altered colour, salience, time and self.

cell protection
;
|
;
altered experience

The two are not equivalent. But their coexistence in the same molecule is another reminder that our divisions between quantity and quality, poison and protection, organism and experience may sometimes be less fundamental than they appear.

Perhaps the evolutionary problem was to detect poison. But the wider problem was to detect when the machinery by which a world is experienced had itself been changed.

The quality/quantity blur becomes especially interesting here.

From inside: too hot

From inside: hunger

The two sides are not equal.

Quantity is difference under a common external measure.

Quality is difference as it matters from somewhere.

Perhaps subject and object begin to appear around the same divide:

Why should some differences merely exist while others seem to exist for something?

Why should temperature become warmth?

Why should chemistry become hunger?

Why should succession become memory and anticipation?

No thermodynamic equation answers that.

But perhaps the question becomes clearer if we stop assuming that subject and object were already there at the beginning.

Time Becomes Handed
For a situated organism, past and future are radically asymmetric.

The past supplies records and constraints.

The future supplies possibilities.

retained past
→
self
→
possible futures
.

I remember in one direction.

I anticipate in the other.

The past is inherited.

The future is acted toward.

Perhaps time becomes something like chiral from within a living trajectory.

Not literally molecular chirality.

But structurally there is a resemblance: a formally available symmetry becomes a persistent orientation.

A self may therefore be understood, at least provisionally, as:

a persistent orientation through a changing space of differences.

Birth is the formation of such an orientation.

Life is its continued maintenance and elaboration.

Death is the ending of that particular continuity.

This does not require consciousness itself to have been produced by the process.

Ātman and Jīva
That is where the wider Advaita lens becomes interesting.

Suppose consciousness is prior to representation rather than produced by it.

Then evolution need not create awareness from nothing.

It creates temporary organisations within which awareness becomes oriented:

this body,

this hunger,

this memory,

this future.

The empirical self—the jīva—can arise and die.

Ātman need not.

The speculative sequence is therefore not necessarily:

matter
→
consciousness
.

It may instead be something like:

difference
→
constraint
→
orientation
→
history
→
self
.

This addresses the emergence of a perspective.

It does not necessarily address the existence of consciousness itself.

The dangerous leap would be to infer Ātman from the machinery.

No hypercube, thermodynamic equation or symmetry-breaking process proves that an organised whole has an inside.

The Upanishads offer a lens through which to ask whether that inside must have been created at all.

Honey and the Holomovement
The Madhu or Honey teaching in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka supplies another image.

The earth is honey for beings.

Beings are honey for the earth.

The pattern repeats with fire, air, sun, directions and other elements of the world.

The explanatory direction becomes reciprocal:

A
↔
B
.

Things do not first exist in complete isolation and then enter into relationships.

Their identities are partly constituted through participation in a larger whole.

This produces a strikingly Bohm-like resonance.

Bohm’s holomovement similarly asks us not to imagine reality fundamentally as independent objects with relations added afterward.

Stable forms can instead be relatively persistent unfoldings within a deeper movement.

An explicate thing is real without being absolutely separate.

A self, from this perspective, might be something like an eddy.

The eddy has a boundary.

A direction.

A history.

It persists for a while.

But its substance is the river.

That seems a better image of a temporary whole than a fragment cut away from something larger.

The river remains river.

The eddy remains real.

The distinction matters.

The separation is not absolute.

The Ever Dividing Whole
So perhaps the basic movement is not:

whole
→
pieces
.

It is:

whole
→
difference
→
constraint
→
orientation
→
temporary whole
→
new difference
.

And perhaps even that is too sequential.

The whole does not first exist and then begin dividing.

Its dividing may be part of how it exists.

The hypercube therefore cannot be fixed.

The dimensions change.

New tensions emerge.

New forms of salience appear.

Temporary wholes acquire worlds.

Some acquire histories.

Some may acquire interiors.

Advaita makes one final, deliberately dangerous suggestion: perhaps consciousness is not the last thing produced by this process. Perhaps what the process produces is a from here within something more fundamental.

That remains speculation.

But suppose for a minute.

Perhaps the dividing does not shatter the whole.

Perhaps it is the whole looking back at itself from a difference it just invented.

Neti; Neti
Bohm repeatedly warned that abstractions from the whole are necessarily incomplete. A physical law may capture a stable order within some domain, but no finite description should be confused with the whole from which it was abstracted.

The Upanishadic refrain neti neti—“not this, not this”—offers a strikingly similar discipline.

Not because the two traditions are saying the same thing, and not because reality must therefore be mystical or inaccessible. The point is simpler:

description
≠
whole
.

Every successful representation leaves something outside its frame.

The hypercube of opposites is no exception. Nor are free-energy descriptions, computational models, theories of consciousness, or the metaphors of implicate and explicate order. Each may reveal a structure. None need exhaust what is structured.

This matters increasingly as human knowledge is supplemented by AI. A sufficiently capable model can compress, connect and manipulate vastly more representations than any individual person. But greater representational power does not abolish the distinction between a model and what it models.

An AI may build a larger map.

It is still a map.

Indeed, the more coherent and comprehensive the map becomes, the greater the temptation to mistake coherence for completeness.

So neti neti can be read not as a retreat from knowledge but as a warning built into knowledge itself:

Not this model. Not this theory. Not even this one.

The whole may be increasingly knowable without ever becoming exhaustively known.
